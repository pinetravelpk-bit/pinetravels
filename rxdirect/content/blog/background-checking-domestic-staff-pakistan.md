Domestic staff have access to your home, your family and often your daily routine. A proper background check isn't about mistrust, it's about making an informed decision, the same way you would for any other important hire.

## What a thorough background check should include

### 1. CNIC and address verification

Confirm the candidate's CNIC is genuine and that the listed address is current. This is the foundation of every other check.

### 2. Reference calls with at least two previous employers

Ask specific questions: Were they punctual? Trustworthy with money or valuables left around? How did they handle being alone in the house? A vague or reluctant answer is a signal worth noting.

### 3. In-person interview

A phone screening isn't enough. Meet candidates in person, ask about their experience in detail, and pay attention to how they answer, not just what they say.

### 4. Role-specific checks

- **Security guards:** a police character certificate should be standard.
- **Drivers:** license authenticity and traffic violation history.
- **Cooks, helpers, nannies:** health screening and, where children are involved, extra reference depth.

### 5. A trial period before long-term commitment

Even after all the above, a short paid trial period lets both sides confirm the fit before a longer-term arrangement begins.

## Documents you should actually ask for

Don't rely on a candidate simply telling you their history, ask for paper. At minimum:

- **Original CNIC**, not a photocopy, so you can compare the photo and details in person before taking a copy for your records.
- **Proof of current address**, such as a utility bill or a family member's CNIC showing the same address, especially if the candidate has moved recently.
- **A local police character certificate**, where available. This is standard for security guards and increasingly common for live-in staff, and it's worth asking for even when it isn't strictly required, a candidate's willingness to get one tells you something on its own.
- **Contact details for at least two previous employers**, not just one, a single reference can be a friend or relative rather than a genuine past employer.

If a candidate is reluctant to provide any of these, or the address on their CNIC doesn't match where they say they're currently living, ask why before moving forward. There's often a reasonable explanation, recent relocation, family property still listed as the CNIC address, but you want to hear it directly rather than assume.

## How to structure a trial period properly

A trial period only works if it's actually structured, not just "let's see how it goes."

- **Set a defined length upfront**, commonly one to two weeks for daily staff, a bit longer for live-in roles, so both sides know when the formal decision point is.
- **Agree on pay for the trial period before it starts.** Staff should be paid fairly for trial days; this isn't unpaid probation.
- **Give specific tasks and observe how they're handled**, not just whether the person shows up. For a cook, that might mean a few different meals. For a helper, a full day's routine including moments when you step out and they're alone in the house.
- **Check in daily during the trial**, briefly, so small issues get raised and corrected early rather than surfacing as a pattern only after the trial ends.
- **Decide and communicate promptly** once the trial period is over, don't leave someone in limbo for weeks after they've technically finished the trial.

## Red flags to watch for in interviews and during the trial

Most candidates are honest and reliable, but a few patterns are worth taking seriously when you see them:

- Vague or shifting answers about why they left their last position, especially if the story changes between the interview and later conversations.
- A previous employer who is hesitant to give a straight answer, or who is reachable but gives clearly rehearsed, overly positive responses with no specifics at all.
- Reluctance to provide original documents, or documents that look altered or inconsistent (mismatched address, unclear photo, etc.).
- During the trial: unexplained absences, evasiveness about where they were during work hours, or discomfort with basic questions about their previous households.
- Requests for advance payment very early in the relationship, before any trust has been established, not automatically a dealbreaker, but worth discussing openly rather than ignoring.

None of these alone is proof of a problem, but two or more together are a reason to slow down and ask more questions before committing long-term.

## Why a phone call beats a written reference letter

Many candidates carry a written reference letter from a previous employer, and it's tempting to treat that as sufficient. It usually isn't, for a simple reason: a letter is written once, often by the departing employer as a courtesy, and it can't be questioned. A phone call can.

When you call a previous employer, ask specific, closed-and-open combinations rather than "was he good?":

- "How long did they work for you, and why did the arrangement end?"
- "Were they ever left alone in the house with valuables or children? How did that go?"
- "Was there ever an issue with money, punctuality, or honesty that you had to address directly?"
- "Would you hire them again if you had the chance?"

A written letter can't react to a follow-up question or a pause before an answer, a phone call can, and that hesitation is often more informative than the words themselves. Wherever possible, get both: the letter as a starting point, and a call to actually verify it.

## Why this matters more in Pakistan's growing cities

As Islamabad, Rawalpindi, Lahore and Karachi continue to grow, more households are hiring staff through informal networks with no verification layer at all. A structured process protects your family and treats the staff member fairly too, clear expectations, fair wages and a documented working relationship benefit everyone.
