A driver spends more unsupervised time with your family than almost any other household hire, school runs, errands, sometimes hours alone with your car. Here are the five questions we recommend asking before any offer is made.

## 1. "Can I see your original driving license?"

Never accept a photocopy alone. Check the license against the CNIC, confirm the issue date lines up with claimed years of experience, and verify it hasn't expired.

## 2. "Have you had any traffic violations or accidents?"

Most candidates will be honest if asked directly and calmly. Cross-check this against their references where possible, a previous employer will usually mention if there were issues.

## 3. "Which areas of Rawalpindi and Islamabad do you know best?"

A driver who can navigate Saddar, Bahria Town, DHA and the twin-city commute routes confidently will save you time every single day. Ask them to describe a specific route to test real familiarity, not memorized answers.

## 4. "What's your availability, and what happens during Ramadan or family emergencies?"

Set expectations on working hours, weekly off-days and how last-minute changes are handled before you finalize anything.

## 5. "Can I speak to your last two employers?"

This is non-negotiable. A driver with genuine experience will have contactable references who can confirm punctuality, driving safety and trustworthiness.

## Verifying licenses and documents properly

A license check should go beyond glancing at the card. Confirm the license category matches the vehicle you're hiring for (car vs. light transport), check the issuing authority and expiry date, and where possible cross-reference the license number independently rather than relying only on the physical document. Ask for the CNIC alongside the license and confirm the names and details match exactly, mismatches, even minor ones, are worth asking about directly rather than dismissing as a clerical error. If the driver has previously worked for a company or corporate fleet, ask for any company-issued ID or experience letter, since that adds a second point of verification beyond personal references.

## How a driver's trial period should work

A short trial, a few days of supervised or semi-supervised driving, tells you more than any interview. Start with familiar, low-stakes routes (school run, local errands) before handing over longer or unsupervised trips. Pay attention to how they handle traffic stress, whether they follow basic safety habits like seatbelt use and speed limits without being reminded, and how they treat the vehicle itself, parking care, fuel discipline, reporting any issue with the car promptly. A driver who's transparent about a small mistake during the trial (a scrape, a wrong turn) is a better long-term sign than one who avoids mentioning it.

## What a typical booking looks like

Most households reach out with their general route needs, school runs, office commute, occasional long trips, and preferred hours. We shortlist two or three verified drivers, usually within 48 hours, matched to the routes and schedule described. The household interviews candidates directly, often starting with a phone call before meeting in person, and a short paid trial follows before any long-term commitment. If the fit isn't right during the trial, we shortlist a replacement rather than leaving the household to start the search over from scratch.

## Red flags worth pausing on

A few patterns are worth a longer conversation before you commit: reluctance to produce an original license or CNIC (only offering a photocopy or photo of it), vague or inconsistent answers about previous employers, an inability to describe specific routes they claim to know well, or a previous job that "just ended" with no clear explanation. None of these alone rules someone out, but two or more together are a reason to slow down and dig further before making an offer.

## How references should actually be verified

A written reference letter is a weak signal on its own, it's easy to produce and hard to challenge. Call the previous employer directly instead. Ask specific, open-ended questions: how long did they actually work for you, how was their timekeeping, did anything ever happen with the car that concerned you, and would you hire them again. A genuine previous employer will usually answer these naturally and in some detail; someone reciting a rehearsed line, or an employer who seems unsure who you're asking about, is worth noting.

## Structuring a fair trial period

A trial should have a clear start and end point agreed before it begins, commonly a week to two weeks, with both sides understanding it's a two-way evaluation, not just the household testing the driver. Start with routes and tasks you can supervise or verify easily, and expand to less-supervised responsibilities only once you're comfortable. Give direct feedback during the trial rather than saving it all for the end; how a driver responds to correction (a note about a hard brake, a request to take a different route) is often as informative as the driving itself.

## What to expect on replacement policy

If a placement doesn't work out, a reasonable agency will offer a replacement from an already-screened pool rather than leaving you to restart the search cold. Ask about this upfront, before you commit to a driver, so you know what happens if the fit isn't right, and confirm whether a replacement comes with the same level of verification (license, references, road test) as the original placement, not a lighter version of it.
