Finding a good cook in Islamabad is easy. Finding a cook you can genuinely trust in your home, around your family and your food, is a different problem entirely. Word-of-mouth referrals can work, but they leave you with no real verification, no background check, no reference call, sometimes not even a confirmed address.

Here's what we recommend checking before you hire, whether you go through an agency or not.

## 1. Verify identity and address

Always ask for a CNIC copy and confirm the address it lists. This is the baseline for any household hire, and it's the first step in RX Direct's screening process for every cook we place.

## 2. Call at least two previous employers

A cook's cooking skill matters, but reliability matters more. Previous employers can tell you about punctuality, hygiene habits, and how they handled being alone in a home. We call every reference before a candidate is shortlisted.

## 3. Run a paid trial before committing long-term

A short trial period, even just a week, tells you more than any interview. Watch for hygiene practices, how they handle your kitchen equipment, and whether they take direction well on dietary preferences.

## 4. Clarify live-in vs. live-out expectations upfront

Islamabad households vary widely in what they expect: some need a live-in cook for round-the-clock meal prep, others just need someone for lunch and dinner. Be explicit about hours, off-days and accommodation (if live-in) before an offer is made.

## 5. Ask about cuisine experience specifically

"Can you cook" is too broad a question. Ask specifically about Pakistani home cooking, continental dishes, or whatever your household needs, and ask for a sample dish during the trial.

## What a trial period actually looks like

A good trial isn't just a taste test. Over the trial days, ask the cook to prepare a full meal cycle, breakfast, lunch and dinner if possible, so you can see how they manage timing, kitchen cleanup between courses, and how they adapt when you give feedback mid-trial ("less oil," "less spice," "my kids don't eat this"). A cook who takes correction well during the trial is usually easier to work with long-term than one who cooks well but bristles at adjustment. It's also worth observing how they handle an unfamiliar kitchen layout and equipment, since that tells you how quickly they'll settle in if hired.

## Red flags to watch for

A few things are worth pausing on during screening or the trial: reluctance to provide a CNIC copy or give a real previous address, inability to name or describe a previous employer in any detail, visible discomfort with basic hygiene practices (handwashing, clean workspace habits), or vague answers about why a previous job ended. None of these alone is disqualifying, but two or more together are worth a longer conversation before you commit.

## Common questions about hiring a cook

**What if our household has specific dietary needs, low oil, diabetic-friendly, or a particular regional cuisine?**
Flag this upfront during the interview and ask for a trial dish that reflects it specifically. A cook with genuine experience in that style of cooking will be able to talk through ingredient substitutions and portion adjustments without hesitation.

**Live-in or live-out, which is more common in Islamabad?**
Both are common, and it usually comes down to household size and schedule. Live-out cooks who come in for set meal times work well for smaller households with a predictable routine; live-in arrangements suit larger households or those needing coverage across long or irregular hours. Either way, agree on off-days and accommodation details before the offer is finalized.

**Should we expect the cook to also handle grocery shopping?**
This varies by household and should be a specific line item in your initial conversation, not an assumption on either side. Some cooks are comfortable managing the kitchen budget and market runs; others prefer to just cook from what's provided. Clarify it early to avoid friction later.
