## Home care staff Pakistan: two roles, different skills

**[Home nurses](/services/nurses)** are trained in medical care. They can:

- Give injections and manage IV drips and cannulas
- Dress wounds and surgical sites, and spot early signs of infection
- Care for catheters, colostomy bags and NG tubes
- Monitor blood pressure, sugar levels, oxygen and temperature
- Manage medicines, including timing and doses for complex prescriptions
- Turn bedridden patients to prevent bed sores
- Recognise when a patient needs to go back to hospital

**[Caretakers](/services/caretakers)** handle daily living. They can:

- Help the patient walk, sit, stand and move between bed and chair
- Bathe, dress and help with toileting
- Feed patients who cannot eat on their own, and prepare simple meals
- Give oral medicines at the right times from a prepared schedule
- Keep the room clean and the bedding fresh
- Accompany the patient to appointments
- Provide company, conversation and watchfulness

Many families start with a nurse after a hospital stay and move to a caretaker once the medical needs reduce. Some need both: a nurse for a few hours each day and a caretaker for the rest.

## Elderly care staff Pakistan: caring for ageing parents

Most of our care placements are for elderly parents. Their needs usually fall into one of three groups:

**Independent but frail.** The parent can walk and eat but needs supervision, company and help with bathing or medicines. A caretaker during the day, or a live-in caretaker, is usually enough.

**Limited mobility or memory loss.** The parent needs help moving, may wander or forget medicines, and cannot be left alone. A live-in caretaker with experience of dementia, or two caretakers on shifts, works best.

**Bedridden or medically complex.** The parent needs turning, feeding, catheter care or regular injections. A home nurse is needed, often with a caretaker for support.

When you hire elderly care staff in Pakistan, tell us honestly how your parent is doing. It helps us send someone who will cope, rather than someone who leaves after a week because the work was more than they expected.

## Caretaker service Pakistan: what a good caretaker looks like

Skill matters, but temperament matters more. The caretakers families keep for years tend to be:

- **Patient.** Elderly people can be slow, confused or irritable. A good caretaker does not rush or argue.
- **Clean.** They keep themselves, the patient and the room hygienic without being told.
- **Observant.** They notice when the patient is eating less, sleeping more or seems in pain, and tell the family.
- **Respectful.** They protect the patient's dignity during bathing and toileting.
- **Honest.** They follow the medicine schedule exactly and tell you if a dose was missed.

During interviews for our caretaker service in Pakistan, we ask scenario questions to test these qualities, and we ask references specifically about patience and honesty.

## Signs a parent may need care at home

Families often notice the signs slowly. A mother who used to cook every day now skips meals. A father who walked to the mosque five times a day now stays in bed until noon. Medicines are found untouched in the drawer. There has been a fall in the bathroom, and nobody found out until the evening. These are not things to wait on.

If your parent lives alone or spends most of the day alone, a caretaker can make a real difference. It is not only about physical help. Many elderly people in Pakistan feel lonely once children move out or move abroad, and a patient, kind caretaker gives them someone to talk to, someone to walk with in the evening and someone who notices when they are unwell. We have seen parents become more active and cheerful within weeks of a good caretaker arriving.

Some families worry that their parent will refuse help. That is common. Our advice is to introduce the caretaker as someone who helps around the house first, and let the relationship build. Most parents accept care once they see it makes daily life easier.

## Dementia and memory loss

Caring for a parent with dementia or Alzheimer's is one of the hardest jobs there is. The person may repeat questions, become suspicious, wander out of the house or not recognise family members. A caretaker for dementia needs extra patience and some specific habits: keeping a fixed daily routine, speaking calmly, removing hazards such as gas knobs and sharp objects, and never arguing with the patient's confusion.

When you tell us your parent has memory loss, we shortlist caretakers who have handled it before and ask references specifically about it. We also suggest that families keep the caretaker the same for as long as possible, since change is especially hard for people with dementia.

## Working alongside the family

Care staff work best when they are part of a team with the family, not a replacement for it. Agree who the caretaker or nurse should report to, and how often. Share the doctor's instructions in writing. Let the worker know which decisions they can make, such as calling an ambulance, and which need a family member. A short weekly conversation about how things are going prevents most problems and makes the worker feel valued.

## Preparing your home for care staff

A few changes make care safer and easier:

1. A bed the patient can get in and out of easily, ideally with a side rail
2. Grab bars and a non slip mat in the bathroom
3. A written medicine schedule on the wall, with doses and times
4. Emergency numbers on the fridge, including the doctor and nearest hospital
5. A comfortable chair and place to rest for the caretaker
6. For live-in staff, a clean private room and proper meals
