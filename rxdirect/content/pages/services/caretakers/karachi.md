## Elderly caretaker Karachi: what a caretaker does

An elderly caretaker in Karachi usually helps with walking and moving safely around the house, getting in and out of bed, chairs and the car, bathing, dressing and grooming, toileting and continence care, preparing simple meals and helping the person eat, giving tablets on time from a medicine box prepared by the family or doctor, light exercises suggested by the doctor or physiotherapist, accompanying the person to doctor visits, the mosque or family gatherings, keeping the room clean and comfortable, and providing company and conversation.

A caretaker does not give injections, manage IV lines or care for feeding tubes and catheters. Those tasks need a qualified [home nurse in Karachi](/services/nurses/karachi).

## Parents living alone in flats

Elderly parents living alone in Karachi flats face particular risks: a fall with nobody to help, lifts that stop during load shedding, difficulty carrying groceries upstairs and loneliness. A live-in caretaker solves most of these. If the parent prefers a day caretaker, ask a neighbour or the building guard to keep an eye out in the evenings, give the parent an easy to use phone with emergency numbers saved and arrange a daily call.

## Male or female caretaker?

Most families choose a female caretaker for a mother and a male caretaker for a father, especially because bathing and toileting are involved. Male caretakers are often preferred for heavier patients. Tell us your preference.

## Caring for parents with dementia

A parent with dementia may forget names, repeat questions, become anxious in the evening, refuse food or try to leave the flat. A good dementia caretaker stays calm, follows a steady routine, gently redirects rather than arguing, keeps doors locked and keeps the family informed.

## Heat, humidity and elderly health

Karachi's heat and humidity are hard on elderly people. During heatwaves, the caretaker keeps the parent indoors in the hottest hours, offers water and juices often, keeps the room cool and watches for dizziness, confusion or very dark urine. Power cuts make this harder, so a UPS for fans is worth having.

## Preventing falls

A caretaker walks beside the person, makes sure they use their walker or stick, keeps floors dry and clear, uses a night light and helps them get up slowly. Grab bars, a raised toilet seat and a non slip mat make a big difference.

## Caretaker, nurse or helper?

A **helper** suits parents who are still largely independent. A **caretaker** suits parents who need hands on help with walking, bathing, toileting and eating. A **nurse** is needed for medical procedures. Many families move from a helper to a caretaker as a parent grows older.

## Food, fluids and medicines

A caretaker offers small, soft meals and drinks through the day, follows any diet the doctor has set and keeps a simple note of what was eaten. Tablets should be given from a weekly pill box filled by a family member, with each dose ticked on a chart.

## Companionship and dignity

A caretaker who sits and talks, takes the parent for a short walk in the building garden or along the seafront on a cool evening, and helps them call family on video does as much good as any medicine. Ask the caretaker to treat your parent with respect and protect their privacy.

## Updates for families abroad

Overseas families can ask the caretaker for a short daily WhatsApp message and a weekly video call with their parent. A trusted relative should visit regularly.

## Signs the caretaker is working well

After the first month, look for simple signs: your parent seems calmer and cleaner, eats better, takes medicines on time, has fewer falls or near misses and talks about the caretaker warmly. The room is tidy, the medicine chart is filled in and the caretaker tells you about small changes without being asked. If you see the opposite, such as unexplained bruises, sudden fear, weight loss or a parent who goes quiet when the caretaker is near, act at once and tell us.

## Keeping a good caretaker

Pay on time, give a weekly day off with relief cover, provide proper meals and a clean place to rest, and treat the caretaker with respect. Help with transport on strike days if you can. A caretaker who feels valued stays longer and gives better care.
