## Elderly caretaker Lahore: what a caretaker does

An elderly caretaker in Lahore usually helps with walking and moving safely around the house, getting in and out of bed, chairs and the car, bathing, dressing and grooming, toileting and continence care, preparing simple meals and helping the person eat, giving tablets on time from a medicine box prepared by the family or doctor, light exercises suggested by the doctor or physiotherapist, accompanying the person to doctor visits, the mosque or family gatherings, keeping the room clean and comfortable, and providing company and conversation.

A caretaker does not give injections, manage IV lines or care for feeding tubes and catheters. Those tasks need a qualified [home nurse in Lahore](/services/nurses/lahore).

## Caring for parents with dementia

A parent with dementia may forget names, repeat questions, become anxious in the evening, refuse food or try to leave the house. A good dementia caretaker stays calm, follows a steady routine, gently redirects rather than arguing, keeps doors and gates secure and keeps the family informed. Share your parent's life story, favourite foods, old songs and routines so the caretaker can connect with them.

## Preventing falls

Falls are a major danger for elderly people. A caretaker walks beside the person, makes sure they use their walker or stick, keeps floors dry and clear, uses a night light and helps them get up slowly. Grab bars, a raised toilet seat, a non slip mat and a ground floor room make a big difference.

## Smog, heat and elderly health

Lahore's smog from November to January is hard on elderly lungs and hearts. A caretaker keeps windows closed on bad days, makes sure the air purifier is running where the family has one, avoids outdoor walks and watches for coughing or breathlessness. In summer, temperatures above 45 degrees and power cuts put elderly people at risk of dehydration and heatstroke, so the caretaker offers water often and keeps the room cool.

## Caretaker, nurse or helper?

A **helper** suits parents who are still largely independent and need company, errands and help around the house. A **caretaker** suits parents who need hands on help with walking, bathing, toileting and eating. A **nurse** is needed for medical procedures. Many families move from a helper to a caretaker as a parent grows older, and add a nurse for a few weeks after a hospital stay.

## Food, fluids and medicines

Elderly people often eat and drink too little. A caretaker offers small, soft meals and drinks through the day, follows any diet the doctor has set and keeps a simple note of what was eaten. Tablets should be given from a weekly pill box filled by a family member, with each dose ticked on a chart.

## Companionship and dignity

Many elderly parents are lonely. A caretaker who sits and talks, reads the newspaper aloud, takes them for a gentle walk in the park or to Friday prayers and helps them call family on video does as much good as any medicine. Ask the caretaker to treat your parent with respect, let them do what they can for themselves and protect their privacy.

## Updates for families abroad

Overseas families can ask the caretaker for a short daily WhatsApp message and a weekly video call with their parent. A trusted relative should visit regularly.

## Signs the caretaker is working well

After the first month, look for simple signs: your parent seems calmer and cleaner, eats better, takes medicines on time, has fewer falls or near misses and talks about the caretaker warmly. The room is tidy, the medicine chart is filled in and the caretaker tells you about small changes without being asked. If you see the opposite, such as unexplained bruises, sudden fear, weight loss or a parent who goes quiet when the caretaker is near, act at once and tell us.
