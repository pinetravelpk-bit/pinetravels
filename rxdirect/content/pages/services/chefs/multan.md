## Cooking in Multan's heat

Multan's summer is one of the hottest in the country, and a busy kitchen in June is punishing. Good chefs prep early in the morning, use slow cooking methods that need less attention, and keep afternoon cooking to a minimum. Families can help with a strong exhaust, a pedestal fan, cold water and a cool room for live-in chefs. Food safety is critical: meat, dairy and cooked food spoil quickly, and buffets at summer events need careful management.

## Weddings, dawats and Eid

Multan weddings often include several days of functions, some held at home. Event chefs with deg and catering experience plan quantities, coordinate helpers and keep food fresh in the heat. Eid brings open houses with guests all day. Book event chefs early. We can arrange [helpers in Multan](/services/helpers/multan) for prep, serving and washing up.

## Mango season

From June to August, Multan's mangoes are everywhere. A creative chef uses them in shakes, kulfi, trifles, chutneys and desserts, and knows how to store them so they ripen well. For families who host during mango season, this is a small but delightful skill, and guests from other cities always remember a Multani mango dessert.

## Landowning families and farmhouse events

Many Multan families own farms in the surrounding districts and host gatherings at farmhouses, especially in winter and at harvest time. Chefs for these events may need to cook outdoors, manage open fires for sajji or BBQ, and work with limited kitchen facilities. Mention farmhouse events in your brief, and we will shortlist chefs comfortable with them. Outdoor cooking needs extra planning: carrying ingredients and equipment, keeping meat cold until cooking, protecting food from dust and flies, and arranging water for washing up. A chef who has done farmhouse events before will think of these details without being asked.

## South Punjab dishes and hospitality

Multan's kitchens draw on a rich regional tradition. Families often expect dishes that are less common in the north: sajji roasted slowly over coals, rich mutton dishes for guests, sohan halwa at special occasions, and the simple, comforting daal and roti that Saraiki households grow up with. Hospitality here is generous and unhurried. Guests are fed until they cannot eat more, and running out of food is unthinkable. A chef in a Multan home needs to cook in generous quantities, keep food hot and fresh as guests arrive through the evening, and serve with warmth. We ask chefs about their experience with these local traditions and test them on the dishes your family considers essential.

## Hosting officials and visiting dignitaries

Multan is a divisional headquarters, and senior officials, politicians and community leaders often host visitors at home. These dinners call for a chef who can produce a polished meal for important guests, manage serving staff, and handle last minute changes in guest numbers calmly. Discretion is essential in these homes. We check references carefully for chefs placed in such households and brief them on conduct and confidentiality.

## Ramadan iftar gatherings

In many Multan homes, Ramadan means hosting iftar for relatives, neighbours and sometimes the wider community. A chef plans iftar spreads that arrive at the table on time, often for large numbers, and manages the heat of a summer Ramadan in the kitchen. With good planning and a helper or two, a chef makes this month of hospitality possible without exhausting the household.

## Healthy eating and special diets

Many Multan elders live with diabetes and heart conditions. A chef can prepare lighter versions of favourite dishes and separate diet meals. Share any medical guidance, and we will shortlist chefs experienced with diet cooking for older family members.

## Sourcing and costs

Good chefs know Multan's markets and suppliers, buy seasonal produce at the right time and plan menus to reduce waste. Agree on a grocery budget and purchase records, and review spending after the first month.

## Live-in chefs and accommodation

Full-time chefs in Multan are often live-in. Provide a clean room with a fan or cooler, bathroom access, proper meals and a weekly day off. In Multan's summer, a cool room is essential for a chef who has spent the day in a hot kitchen.

## Working with existing staff

If you already have a cook, decide whether the chef leads the kitchen or handles events. Clear roles prevent friction. In many Multan households, the existing cook continues with everyday family meals while the chef takes charge of dawats, weddings and special occasions, and the two work side by side on big days. Others make the chef head of the kitchen, with the cook as a trusted assistant who knows the family's preferences. Either way, introduce the chef properly, explain the arrangement to both, and give the existing cook credit for what they already do well.

## Questions to ask in the interview

Ask how they would plan a dawat for fifty, how they manage food safety in Multan's heat, how they cook sajji or deg biryani, and what they would do if a dish failed before guests arrived. Practical, specific answers show real experience. A chef who has handled big Multan gatherings will talk easily about quantities, deg sizes, timings and how many helpers are needed.
