## Furniture carpenter Pakistan: choosing materials

A good furniture carpenter in Pakistan will help you choose materials that suit your budget and climate:

**Solid wood (sheesham, deodar, kail, oak).** Strong, long lasting and repairable. Sheesham is popular for furniture; deodar is naturally resistant to insects. More expensive, and needs seasoning to avoid warping.

**Plywood.** Strong and stable, good for cabinets and wardrobes. Marine ply resists moisture better and suits kitchens and bathrooms.

**MDF.** Smooth and affordable, good for painted or laminated finishes. Swells if water gets in, so edges must be sealed.

**Chipboard (particle board).** The cheapest option, used in flat-pack furniture. Not ideal for kitchens or heavy loads.

**Laminate and veneer.** Surface finishes applied to boards. Laminate is durable and easy to clean; veneer gives a natural wood look.

Ask the carpenter to show you samples and explain the trade-offs.

## Monsoon and termites

Two enemies of wood in Pakistan are moisture and termites. During monsoon, wooden doors and windows swell and stick. A carpenter can plane them, but the lasting fix is proper sealing with polish or paint on all sides, including the top and bottom edges. Termites attack wood in contact with damp walls or floors. Signs include mud tunnels, hollow sounding wood and fine dust. Treat termites before replacing woodwork, or the new wood will be attacked too.

## Common carpentry problems we fix

**Sagging cupboard shelves.** Usually caused by thin boards or long spans without support. A carpenter can add a middle support, replace the board with thicker ply or fix a metal bracket underneath.

**Kitchen cabinet doors hanging crooked.** Hinges loosen with daily use, especially on MDF where screws lose grip. Modern adjustable hinges and proper fixing restore alignment, and in badly worn holes the carpenter can plug and re-drill.

**Drawers that stick or fall out.** Old wooden runners wear down. Replacing them with metal ball bearing channels makes drawers smooth and strong.

**Loose chair and table joints.** Glue dries out over years. Taking the joint apart, cleaning it and re-gluing with clamps is a proper fix; adding nails is not.

**Damaged veneer or laminate.** Edges peel when water gets underneath. Small areas can be re-glued and sealed; larger areas may need a new surface.

**Doors that will not lock.** Often the door has dropped slightly on its hinges, so the latch no longer meets the strike plate. Re-hanging or adjusting the strike plate fixes it.

## Caring for wooden furniture

Good woodwork lasts longer with a little care. Keep wooden furniture away from direct sunlight and air conditioner vents, which dry and crack wood. Wipe spills immediately, especially on MDF and veneer. Use coasters and mats under hot dishes. Polish solid wood furniture every year or two to keep it sealed against moisture. Check regularly for termite signs near walls and floors. For kitchens, never leave wet cloths hanging over cabinet doors, since constant moisture swells edges.

## Warning signs of a poor carpenter

Watch for these signs when choosing a carpenter: no visit to measure before quoting for new work, a large advance requested before materials are bought, vague answers about what wood or board will be used, putty used heavily to hide gaps, and missed deadlines with constant excuses. A carpenter who explains materials clearly, shows samples and gives a realistic timeline is usually more reliable than one who promises everything quickly and cheaply.

## Workshop or on site?

Some carpentry is best done in a workshop, such as new furniture with fine joinery or polish work that needs a dust free space. Other jobs, like fitted wardrobes and kitchen installation, are measured and partly built on site. Ask the carpenter where the work will be done. Workshop work usually gives a better finish, while on site work reduces transport and fits odd spaces more easily.
